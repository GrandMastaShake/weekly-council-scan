# Portfolio check-up

Input is usually a brokerage statement (PDF or screenshot), a CSV export, or
his investing journal. Do not repeat account numbers that appear on it.

## Steps

1. **Date the data.** What date is the statement or file as of? Say it in the
   first line. If it is more than a week old, say what that means for the
   numbers below and ask whether he has a fresher one.
2. **Prices.** Either use the statement's values and say "as of the
   statement date", or search current closes and date each one. Do not mix
   the two in one table without saying so.
3. **The table** (compute with code):

   | Ticker | Shares | Value | % of portfolio | Gain / loss $ | Gain / loss % | Sector |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

4. **Concentration.** Largest position as a % of the total, the top three
   together, and each sector's share. Then the same-bet groups: holdings that
   would all be wrong for the same reason (same sector, same driver, same
   price line on the same index).
5. **The next two weeks.** For each holding: earnings dates and ex-dividend
   dates. Plus market-wide events (Fed, CPI, jobs report).
6. **Plans on file.** If the journal is available, which holdings have a
   written thesis and a "proven wrong" line, and which do not? Holdings with
   no plan are the first thing to fix.
7. **Cash.** Cash as a % of the total, against the target in his profile.
8. **Bottom line, at the top of your answer:** the three things most worth
   his attention, ranked, each one sentence.

## Tone

This is a check-up, not a makeover. Point out what is worth a look and ask
questions. Do not hand him a new portfolio. If something is genuinely
dangerous (one stock at half the account, a margin balance he did not
mention), say it first and say it plainly.

## Output

Offer an updated Holdings sheet for his investing journal with the new
prices, their dates and sources filled in.
